Contractor websites

Roofing contractor website checklist: make requesting a quote straightforward

A homeowner needs to know whether you handle their problem, serve their area and can be trusted with their property. Build your website around those decisions.

Xender Secrets · Published 14 October 2026 · Updated 14 October 2026

Start with the enquiry you want to receive

A roofing website does not need to impress another designer. It needs to help a homeowner explain a leak, compare repair options or arrange an inspection. Before choosing a layout, write down your main services, the places you actually cover and how you want customers to contact you. Those three decisions should shape the first screen and the main navigation.

For example, a small contractor might prioritise roof repairs, reroofing and flat roofs across a defined set of towns. The first screen can state those services, link to recent work and offer a clear “Request a roof inspection” button. Avoid claiming emergency availability if nobody answers outside working hours. A specific, accurate offer is more useful than a broad promise that the business cannot consistently fulfil.

Answer four questions on the first screen

  • What work do you undertake?
  • Which areas do you serve?
  • What evidence can a customer inspect?
  • How do they request a quote or call you?

Use your own project photograph where you have permission, a readable headline and one primary action. On a phone, the business name, service and contact action should be easy to find without dismissing pop-ups or waiting for an animation. A secondary call button can help someone who prefers speaking, but the page should still have a clear main route.

Separate services when the questions differ

Roof repairs and a complete roof replacement involve different questions. A repairs section might explain the symptoms you inspect, what details to send and how an inspection is arranged. A replacement page might explain materials you work with, how you assess scope and what the written quotation covers. Describe your actual process rather than promising a universal price before seeing the property.

A compact site can start with Home, Services, Projects and Contact. Add individual service pages when you can give each one useful, distinct information. Do not create dozens of town pages by replacing the location name in otherwise identical text. One honest service-area section is a better starting point than pages that suggest offices or local experience you do not have.

Make a project gallery useful

A gallery becomes more informative when every photograph has context. For a project you can disclose, describe the type of property, the roofing problem, the work completed and the materials used. Include before-and-after photographs if they accurately show the same job. Get the owner's permission before showing identifiable properties, names or addresses, and avoid publishing exact residential addresses.

A sample caption could be: “Flat-roof replacement on a domestic extension. Removed the worn covering and installed the agreed new roofing system.” Add location or warranty details only when verified and authorised. If your site currently contains concept designs, label them clearly as concepts. Stock photographs and fictional examples should never be presented as your completed customer work.

Use a short quote request form

The first form should collect enough information to respond, rather than attempting to produce a final quote automatically. Useful fields are name, a preferred contact method, a broad area or postcode and a short description of the problem. Make extra detail optional unless it changes how you handle the enquiry. Explain why any location information is needed.

Photo uploads can help assess a request, but they add storage, privacy and file-handling requirements. Start with a reliable contact form and ask for photographs during follow-up if that fits your process. Never encourage customers to climb onto a roof to take pictures. A website form should help arrange assessment, not imply that a remote photograph proves a building is safe.

After submission, show an acknowledgement stating the next step and your actual response hours. Send the request to an inbox somebody checks. If an email or phone field is required, make the error understandable and preserve what the visitor already entered. Test a successful request and a failed request before launch.

Show trust details you can substantiate

Publish your real trading name and business contact details. If you hold relevant accreditations, insurance or trade memberships, check what you are permitted to display and keep the details current. Link to genuine review profiles where appropriate. Explain any workmanship guarantee accurately, including its scope and exclusions, instead of using an unsupported “fully guaranteed” badge.

Do not invent review scores, customer counts or years of experience. If the business is new, useful project photographs, a clear process and an accountable contact person can still help a customer evaluate it. A website should make checking the business easier. It should not replace a written quotation or the customer's normal checks before commissioning building work.

Set up search basics without promising rankings

Give each page a descriptive title, a clear heading and useful text about the service it covers. Connect the services, projects and contact pages with readable links. Keep one preferred address for each page and include indexable pages in the sitemap. These are foundations for discovery; they are not a guarantee of a particular Google position or enquiry volume.

Google's SEO Starter Guide explains how links, page organisation and useful content support discovery. Its helpful-content guidance supports writing for readers rather than manufacturing pages solely for search traffic. Use customer questions to choose future articles, such as what information to prepare before a roof inspection.

Run a practical launch check

  1. Open every page on a phone and check readable text and navigation.
  2. Try the call and email links and confirm they reach the intended business.
  3. Submit a clearly labelled test enquiry and confirm receipt and acknowledgement.
  4. Check service areas, hours, project permissions and all trust claims.
  5. Check that large photographs do not make pages unnecessarily slow.
  6. Confirm ownership of the domain, website files and accounts at handover.

Track enquiries before buying more traffic

Keep a simple record of the enquiry date, service, area, source, response and next action. Review which requests became inspections and quotations, and which became paid work. Avoid storing unnecessary property details in a shared sheet. The purpose is to see where follow-up stalls and which pages attract relevant enquiries, rather than celebrating visits alone.

For the website brief, list the pages, contact route, content you will supply, revisions and handover requirements.
